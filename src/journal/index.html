August 14, 2026: Operating QRP while long distance bicycle touring 🔗

This summer I did my second long-distance bicycle tour. I was 60 days on the road to bike from my hometown in Iowa to Vancouver / Seattle. I ended up riding 2,434 miles through wide plains and mountain ranges, on highways, trails, and gravel / forest roads. It was tough and awesome.

This time I took the QMX transmitter that I can power off a power bank, a vertical telescoping antenna, a end fed wire antenna, and coax, so I could do some operation on my trip. With 6 operations (20 SSB QSOs and 6 FT8 QSOs) it was pretty slim and in hindsight not worth it for me to lug the equipment along. Especially since having a lighter load would have made my ride a little easier. Operating QRP is just extremely hard and I'm not super skilled at it. Once you find a free frequency, because of the low power, other stations don't hear you and start using the frequency for their operations. Forget about operating on weekends when propagation is halfway good, you won't be able to find or keep a frequency at all on those days. In general, the weak signal doesn't get heard much even when spotting yourself as a POTA station. So there's that. Then there is the touring aspect. I did long days and often wasn't up to putting the effort in to unpack, setup, tear down, and repack the station. Besides, there are quite a few other things one has to do on tour before sleeping. On off days, besides resting and doing chores, I wanted to explore the location and meet people.

Going forward, QRP operations will be more dedicated to their own activities and not be included as a small side quest in other activities like bicycle touring.

May 20, 2026: Operating as a US citizen in Canada 🔗

I might have the opportunity to operate in Canada. Probably only QRP and most likely POTA on HF. To prep for that I did a bit of research on the reciprocity between the two countries.

The Treaty Series 1952 No. 7 — Operation of Certain Radio Equipment or Stations established the current reciprocal operating agreement for amateur radio between Canada and the USA in article III.

Key Points:
  • Must be a US citizen (permanent residents can't operate under this agreement)
  • Cannot be a Canadian citizen (dual citizenship with US and Canada)
  • You must use the Canadian callsign if you have one
  • Carry with you your US passport or US passport card
  • Carry with you the official printout of your FCC amateur radio license
  • Must ID with your US callsign and current Canadian call area (e.g. AE0S/VE4)
  • You must indicate your geographical location during each QSO (nearest city and province).
  • You have the lesser of both the US and the Canadian operating privileges
  • You must obey both countries' rules.

This document state that "each visiting amateur may be required to register and receive a permit before operating any amateur station licensed by his government." RIC-3 — Information on the Amateur Radio Service in section 8 further clarifies that "Visiting amateurs are not required to register or receive a permit prior to operating amateur radio stations."

Both documents states that "each amateur station shall indicate its geographical location as nearly as possible by city and state or city and province at least once during each contact with another station."

The treaty states that visiting amateurs need to ID either by radiotelegraph operation with "the amateur call sign issued to him by the licensing country followed by a slant (/) sign and the amateur call sign prefix and call area number of the country he is visiting." or by radiotelephone operation "the amateur call sign in English issued to him by the licensing country followed by the words "fixed", "portable" or "mobile", as appropriate, and the amateur call sign prefix and call area number of the country he is visiting." Both the ARRL and the RAC state that identification is done via the call sign followed by the call area suffix.

Since the treaty is between the USA and Canada, you are still operating under the FCC rules and regulations while in Canada and also having to conform to the Canadian rules and regulations. Refer to the FCC Part 97 Rules, the Canadian Radiocommunication Regulations, and the Canadian RBR-4. For example while Canadians can do voice operation between 14.112 and 14.150 the FCC never granted us voice privileges there. So even during a visit to Canada, a US operator isn't allowed to operate voice on these frequencies.

While there is no need for paperwork or other formalities when visiting Canada, it is recommended to have documentation that proves that you are a US citizen (passport, passport card, or birth certificate) and that you are a licensed by the FCC to operate amateur radios.

January 5, 2020: Getting a new Vanity Callsign 🔗

The grass is greener on the other side of the fence. So when I saw that the AE0S callsign came up for grabs I went for it. It's attractive because it's a group A call sign, tiny bit less weight on phone and morse, maybe better than the "TET" that often gets confused on HF and easier to say on FM. But mostly because the grass was greener.

Having gone through a callsign change before one would think I'd knew the in and outs. Well again I was fortunate to get an uncontested callsign on my first try. I primarily thank AE7Q for that. It's a really invaluable tool for getting vanity callsigns. The weird thing was that the daily data files that 3rd party callsign lookup sites consume only had my K0TET cancellation but not the new callsign assignment. The official ULS did have my new assigned callsign. It took two and a half days for the data files with my new calls to be published and trickled to the 3rd party sites. Something to keep in mind that these sites are not official and will contain at least some incorrect data.

With the new callsign comes the chore of updating everything. My recommendation is to ignore all the web sites for a week or so in order for them to catch up. Hey, it's amateur radio. Nothing is dependent on the internet beside getting your FCC license. So go on the air and confuse people.

Here is a summary of some services and how a callsign change is handled:

  • 3905 Century Club Bureau
    Email the 3905 Century Club Bureau and let them know of your callsign change. Then mention to NCS that you're GIB under the old call until your new call shows up.
  • ARRL Incoming Bureau
    Ask your specific bureau via email what needs to be done. Since my bureau has different sorters for "K" and "S" I need to purchase separate credits for my new call.
  • ARRL Member Website
    Change the username at the account settings page.
  • ARRL Logbook of the World (LotW)
    Per instructions at their FAQ use the TQSL program and go to "Callsign Certificate" -> "Request a new Callsign Certificate" and choose "My new personal callsign (I have a Callsign Certificate for my former callsign)". Fill out the required fields and sign with your old callsign certificate.
  • ARRL Volunteer Examiner (VE)
    If you are a ARRL volunteer examiner you may continue using the ARRL VE badge with the old call sign. However, in that case you must carry a copy of your FCC license with the new call sign. Sign all forms with the new call sign. You can request a replacement badge for a fee. See the section under "Vanity Call Signs" of the VE Manual for details.
  • EchoLink
    Echo link will send you an email stating that the call sign change has been detected and your registration has been automatically transferred to the new call sign. Sign in with your new call sign and the old password. You do need to change the call sign in the EchoLink software.
  • eHam
    Change the username at the account settings page.
  • HamQTH
    So far I was unable to claim the new callsign.
  • HRDLOG
    Change the call sign at the Your account" -> "Utilities (options)" page. Use your new callsign and old password to sign in
  • RemoteHams
    Upload your new official FCC license to the "MyAccount" -> "License Verification" page and post a request in the "General Support" forum to have your callsign updated. In order to upload the license you need to convert the pdf to an image. I did this by printing the pdf to a new first page only pdf. Now that new pdf can be exported as a jpg. Reducing the jpg to 20% seems sufficient.
  • Skeddr
    Click on top right icon, choose "Edit Profile" and update callsign
  • QRZ
    For USA hams this should happen automatic. For others go the QRZ Customer Support Center and sign in with your old callsign and password and use the "Callsign change" link to update the call sign. A request through via the "Contact QRZ support..." link at the QRZ Support site was needed to fix things up for me.
  • QRZCQ
    Go to the "Account" page and click "RENAME" on "Main call"

February 27, 2018: MSK144 Ground Wave Scatter 🔗

This morning I saw some traffic including local on the 6m band (PSK Reporter). Being very excited I wanted to check it out. Switched to MSK144, the mode people were using, and started listening. I heard the local station N0AN having a QSO. I decided to call CQ since he didn't call CQ after his QSO. At the end of the exchange I sent him the non traditional "FIRST MSK144" message. He responded with his phone number and so we ended up chatting for quite a while. I got some very good tips about operating MSK144:

  • 15 second transmit/receive windows
    WSJT-X was still set to 30 seconds. But it should be set to "T/R 15 s"
  • Frequency tolerance of 200
    WSJT-X was still set to 20 but my computer can handle much better than that. The green "Receiving ##%" status bar on the bottom left helps to determine how high the "F Tol" and decoding depth settings can be set. Suggested settings are "Decode -> Deep" and "F Tol 200".
  • TX even/1st toward East and odd/2nd toward west
    This is a convention to follow. Also operate at the same time sequence as other local station, because while you transmit the other station's receiver will be overloaded. Same applies to you, you won't receive anything but the other local station.
  • Noise blanker is OK
    There is no issue with using the noise blanker aggressively
  • Run full power
    I was transmitting at about 30W. Unlike with other data modes here you're better off transmitting with as much power you have available. 100W apparently is considered low power.
  • Ping Jockey
    Monitor and schedule QSOs with Ping Jockey. It's a very useful real time chat for MSK144.
  • Activity is in the mornings
    Best time for QSOs are in the mornings (5/6am). That's when it's most likely that meteors and hams hang around.
  • Read the manual
    Tip from me, it's pretty good. WSJT-X Manual
  • Antenna
    My dipole is probably the worst antenna except for a dummy load but I might be able to talk to some of the better stations.

February 11, 2018: SEA 1625 Antenna Tuner 🔗

I came across the SEA 1625 automatic antenna tuner for a really good price on ebay. The seller mentioned that it's in good working condition but nothing more for the auction. I tried finding some information on the Internet about it but no luck. So I inquired with SeaComCorp.com about the tuner. I got the really helpful and quick answer back:

The 1625 was a slave tuner, made to operate only with the model 225 SSB radio. All of its detection, processing, and memory are performed inside the 225 radio, and the tuner operates off of serial commands from the radio that are series-parallel converted in the tuner to drive the element relays.
If you don't have a 225 series of SSB radio, the tuner is useless. If you do have a 225, the manual is highly recommended.

I forwarded the response to the ebay seller so that they have an idea of what they are selling. But of course the seller didn't care and instead was irritated by me contacting them. Other people have started a bidding war. I hope they know what they are getting because it's not a small ammount of money anymore.

This whole thing brings me to the fact that used amateur radio equipment is overpriced and lots of people seem to take advantage of that and sell untested and questionable equipment for high prices. It's probably a very profitable business for them. And yes, that includes licensed amateur radio guys that could easily test the equipment.

August 20, 2017: Net control 🔗

With the upcoming eclipse the scheduled net control operator was out of town and nobody else has volunteered to run the local net on the 2m repeater. I took the opportunity to try my hand with it, called out if we had a net control and if not volunteered myself.

The tricky parts for me was to log everybody's call sign as I haven't really burned any of them into my brain. The other thing is that I'm way to much on HF and am used to the international radiotelephony alphabet. On top of that I wasn't expecting 13 check ins.

Running the net turned out not to difficult since I could follow the published script and read of some of the upcoming events. Hardest part would have been accepting formal traffic like an ARRL radiogramm and routing it appropriately. Luckily there was no formal traffic.

I enjoyed having had this experience. The next thing I'll have to do sometime is to send send an radiogram to somebody but I haven't figured out to whom. I did receive a radiogram already after I passed my technician and general tests while I checked into the net of the neighbouring town. That was a surprise and pretty awesome.

May 12, 2017: First Portable Operation 🔗

Over lunch hour I went to a local park to test out the new to me Yaesu FT-857D as a portable station on the 10 meter band. I set up a home made dipole vertically with the help of a kite pole and bicycle repair stand. My wife was at home on the Icom IC-7300 and a horizontal dipole.

First contact was on 29.600 Mhz, the FM simplex calling frequency. The strange thing was that after my wife was done talking there was another voice transmission. That happened several times and finally I heard a call sign and decided to talk directly with him. As it turned out I could hear that third station but, most likely because of antenna polarization, my wife didn't hear that station. It was pretty neat to hear another station on 10m since so far I haven't heard any activity on that band.

We played around a bit more on the band. Trying out different modes (AM, SSB) and seeing how low power we could go before loosing any quality with FM (about 5 watts). My lunch hour exploration barely made a dent into the jump start battery that I was using.
